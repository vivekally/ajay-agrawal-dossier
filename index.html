01.1Who he is

The short version

Agrawal is a strategy and innovation economist who has been on the Rotman faculty since 2003. His early academic reputation was built on university-to-industry knowledge transfer: how ideas actually leave a lab, and why patents capture so little of what moves.

In 2012 he founded the Creative Destruction Lab, a not-for-profit mentoring program for seed-stage science companies that takes no equity and charges no fee. It is now the largest thing attached to his name by dollar value.

From 2016 onward, with Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb, he produced the "prediction machines" trilogy that reframed AI as an economics problem rather than a computer science one.

The one idea

Artificial intelligence is not intelligence. It is a drop in the cost of prediction. Everything else follows from that price change. The central claim of Prediction Machines, restated in every subsequent book

The move is deliberately unglamorous: take a technology surrounded by hype, classify it as an input whose price fell, and then apply first-year microeconomics. Cheaper input means you use more of it, you use it in places you would not have bothered before, substitutes lose value, and complements gain value.

The whole body of work is an extended answer to one follow-up question: what are the complements?

03.6The vocabulary

AI as cheap prediction

Prediction defined as "taking information you have to generate information you don't have." Not intelligence, not consciousness, not general reasoning. An input.

Judgment

The act of assigning value or payoff to each possible outcome. Prediction gives you probabilities; judgment tells you what they are worth. Machines got cheap at the first and not the second, so judgment rose in value.

Reward function engineering

The newly valuable human job of specifying the payoffs that map predictions to actions. Coined in the authors' 2017 writing and carried into the book.

Prediction by exception

Machines handle the routine, high-confidence mass of predictions; humans take the rare low-confidence exceptions. The human role gets smaller in volume and higher in stakes, which raises the value of judgment rather than eliminating it.

Data as the complement

"Data is the new oil" gets its own chapter. Three distinct kinds matter: input data to run a prediction, training data to build it, feedback data to improve it. Unique data is the defensible asset; Google and Meta sell predictions, not raw data.

Tasks, not jobs

AI operates on tasks. Jobs are bundles of tasks. So jobs get redesigned rather than deleted, and the redesign question is which bundle of remaining tasks still makes a coherent job.

Known unknowns

Borrowed from Rumsfeld, with "unknown knowns" added. Used to bound where machine prediction works. Machines are weak precisely where history is thin, which is where rare and catastrophic events live.

Strategy, not just productivity

Cheaper prediction can change scale, scope, vertical integration, and investment timing. That is why Part Four puts AI in the C-suite rather than the IT budget.

03.7Structure

Five parts forming a pyramid: prediction, then decision making, then tools, then strategy, then society. The order is an argument in itself. You are not supposed to reach the strategy chapters until you accept the reclassification in Part One.

03.8What a manager is supposed to do with it

  • Reframe every AI question as two questions: what prediction would help here, and what would we actually do differently once we had it.
  • Use the AI Canvas to decompose a specific decision before scoping any model. Name the action first.
  • Invest in the complements, because they are what became scarce: judgment, and data you uniquely hold.
  • Redesign workflows and jobs task by task, and plan for a prediction-by-exception division of labour rather than full automation.
  • Treat AI as a C-suite question about scale, scope, and timing, and manage the new risk surface: liability, discrimination, data security, and being deliberately gamed by the people your model scores.

Part Five: Beyond Business

The closing part steps outside the firm: jobs and inequality, since automation can depress some wages while raising returns to capital and to judgment; competition policy and the risk of data-rich incumbents; privacy traded against performance; and the geopolitics of a US and China race. It is the least developed part of the book and the part its sharpest critic attacks hardest. See the Reception tab.

04.3Infographic: the Between Times

The book's name for the interval between proving a technology works and redesigning the world around it. The anchor case is electrification. Edison's Pearl Street station opened in 1882. Factories did not reorganise around electricity until the 1920s, because the gain was never cheaper power. It was the freedom to abandon the central steam engine, the overhead line shaft, and the building layout that shaft demanded. That took roughly forty years and mostly required new factories rather than converted ones.

The intellectual lineage the book cites: Stanford economist Paul David's 1990 paper The Dynamo and the Computer, itself a response to Robert Solow's line that the computer age was visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The authors' contribution is to say that the lag is not inertia or stupidity. It is that system redesign redistributes power, and the people holding the power are the ones being asked to approve the redesign.

04.4Infographic: rules are glue

A rule is a decision made in advance. Rules are cheap and reliable, and they exist because real-time decisions used to require expensive prediction. But once a rule is in place, an entire system accretes around it. The book calls that surrounding structure scaffolding, built to hide the uncertainty that made the rule necessary. Airport design is the worked example: the terminal is scaffolding for the rule "arrive early".

04.5Infographic: the Great Decoupling, and Flint

For all of history, prediction and judgment arrived bundled inside one human head. AI splits them. Once split, they can be supplied by different people, in different places, at different times, and one person's judgment can be scaled across millions of predictions. That is where the value comes from, and it is also where the power fight starts, because decoupling moves decision rights away from whoever used to hold both halves.

Who resists, and why it is rational

The book's most useful counterintuitive claim is that resistance to a fairer system comes disproportionately from people the unfair system was quietly favouring, and that they are usually not aware of it in those terms. Major League Baseball's automated ball-and-strike calls are the illustration: the most effective resistance came from star players, because human umpire bias had been giving them larger or smaller strike zones depending on which helped them.

The corollary for executives: if nobody inside your organisation objects to your AI plan, it is probably a point solution.

04.7The vocabulary

Power

Used in the strict economic sense: you have power if what you own or control is scarce relative to demand. Not charisma, not hierarchy. System redesign changes what is scarce, which is why it moves power at the industry, company, and individual job level simultaneously.

There is no such thing as a machine decision

Machines do not have power because they do not decide. AI supplies a prediction; codified human judgment turns it into an action. What AI changes is who supplies the judgment and when and where the decision happens.

Decision rights

Assign the right to decide to whoever will decide in the organisation's interest at the lowest cost. Four factors govern it: information, skills, incentives, and coordination. Cheap prediction changes the first, which is why it unsettles the other three.

Thinking probabilistically

AI converts deterministic systems into probabilistic ones, which societies tolerate badly. The authors float an FDA-style regulatory function as the mechanism for making probabilistic systems socially acceptable.

Reliability, coordination, modularity

The two ways to keep a system reliable while introducing probabilistic decisions into it: coordinate the parts tightly, or modularise so a wrong prediction is contained. Chapter 16 is the design chapter.

The flywheel

Because models learn from use and the marginal cost of one more prediction approaches zero, early advantages compound. That is the economic case for racing, and it is why incumbents are told to disrupt themselves before an entrant does.

Complexity arithmetic

Three binary decisions give eight combinations. Ten give 1,024. Twenty give over a million. This is the argument for simulation and digital twins: you cannot reason your way through a redesigned system by hand.

AI bias (epilogue)

Point-solution AI can amplify bias. Their argument is nonetheless that AI belongs in high-stakes decisions because it is more scrutable than a human: you can ask a model thousands of counterfactual questions. They cite Sendhil Mullainathan on algorithmic discrimination being easier to detect and repair than human bias.

04.9What a leader is supposed to do with it

For executives

  • Adopt a system mindset. Task-level thinking can only ever produce point solutions.
  • Run the Discovery Canvas from the mission and a blank slate, not from your current org chart.
  • Expect the power shift and plan for the resistance, including your own.
  • Design for reliability through coordination or modularity, and instrument for high-frequency feedback data.
  • Move early, because learning compounds.

For policymakers

  • Reduce regulatory ambiguity, which is itself a brake on system redesign.
  • Use government's role as a large buyer and as a coordinator to unstick systems no single firm can redesign alone.
  • Design systems so bias can be detected and reduced, rather than banning the model and keeping the unauditable human.

06.2How it works, and why the design matters

Structure

  • Founded 2012 at Rotman, by Agrawal
  • Mission to enhance the commercialization of science for the betterment of humankind
  • Model not-for-profit, objectives-based mentoring for massively scalable, seed-stage, science-based companies
  • Cost to founders no equity taken, no fee charged
  • CEO Sonia Sennik, CDL-Global. Headquarters Toronto
  • Original goal $50 million in graduate equity value within five years
  • Notable mentor astronaut Chris Hadfield, Space stream

Why an economist built it this way

CDL is a working application of his own research. The knowledge-transfer papers found that most of what leaves a university does not travel through patents or licences. It travels through people and relationships. The papers on labour mobility and social proximity found that knowledge flows follow personal ties even after the people move away.

CDL is that finding turned into an institution: put scientists in a room with people who have built companies, force a hard objective every eight weeks, and let the mentors' willingness to keep showing up act as the selection mechanism. Taking no equity removes the financial filter from that judgment.

07.2The serious critique

Laurence Kotlikoff of Boston University, writing in the Journal of Economic Literature 60(3), September 2022, pages 1052 to 1057, under the title "Does Prediction Machines Predict Our AI Future? A Review." DOI 10.1257/jel.20191519. He calls the book a must-read for business leaders, economists, and the public, and then makes six objections.

1. They never define AI

His characterisation of the authors' position: we cannot say what AI is, but we know it when we see it. A book built on reclassifying a technology should be able to say what it is reclassifying.

2. "AI is all about prediction" is overstated

New prediction-based systems build on decades of older AI that was not prediction. The frame is powerful precisely because it is narrow, and the narrowness is not acknowledged.

3. They dismiss the mundane

Digital payments and similar unglamorous systems get short shrift. Kotlikoff attributes this to the authors' own vantage point, naming the Toronto incubator they run, whose companies are selected to focus on prediction.

4. Complacency about jobs

He characterises the stance as a "what, me, worry?" attitude, supported by strained examples of the new human jobs that are supposed to appear.

5. No macroeconomics

The book works at the level of the firm and the task. It has nothing to say about aggregate demand, the distribution of returns to capital, or the possibility of long-run immiseration.

6. State control is underplayed

His sharpest move is to reframe the book against itself: read properly, he says, it is actually a terrifying book, about surveillance and behavioural manipulation, that does not know it.

Why this critique lands. Objections 1, 2, and 3 are about scope, and the authors would likely accept them as the price of a deliberately simple frame. Objections 5 and 6 are different in kind. They say the frame is not merely narrow but systematically excludes the effects most likely to matter, and that the exclusions run in one direction: toward optimism. Notably, Power and Prediction answers none of these directly. It moves to system design and power inside industries, which is a step toward objection 6 without engaging it.

07.3Reader-level criticism

Prediction Machines

Repetitive, and thin on technical depth. Both are fair and largely deliberate: it is a non-technical book that restates one idea in many settings.

Power and Prediction

Repetitive toward the end. Focused on large enterprises rather than small and mid-size firms, which is a real gap given that the adoption data shows firm size is the strongest predictor of AI use. Light on ethical risk, and silent on the disruption of creative industries, which aged poorly given what happened after November 2022.

07.4Where the argument is most exposed

Three observations that follow from the material on this page rather than from any named reviewer. Flagged as such.

  • The ship-then-shop example never materialised. Eight years after the thought experiment, anticipatory shipping remains a patent rather than a business model. The second book concedes the example needed a system solution, which converts a prediction about Amazon into a restatement of the new framework. That is a defensible move, but it is not a confirmed forecast. Inference
  • The electrification analogy is load-bearing and unfalsifiable in the short run. If AI transforms industries, the Between Times framework predicted it. If it does not, the framework says we are still in the Between Times. The analogy is genuinely illuminating and it also cannot lose, which is worth holding in mind. Inference
  • Generative AI arrived between the two books. ChatGPT launched two weeks after Power and Prediction published. The prediction frame accommodates it, since next-token prediction is literally prediction, but the most visible consequences since have been in generation, creative work, and interface rather than in the decision-system redesign the book is about. Inference
