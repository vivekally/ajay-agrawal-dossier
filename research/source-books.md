# Prediction Machines and Power and Prediction: A Detailed Comparative Summary

## TL;DR
- **"Prediction Machines" (2018, updated 2022) reframes AI as a drop in the cost of *prediction*, and shows that when prediction gets cheap, businesses use more of it, the value of its complements (human *judgment* and *data*) rises, and firms must rethink tasks, workflows, and strategy.** Its signature tools are the "anatomy of a decision" (prediction, judgment, action, outcome, plus input/training/feedback data) and the "AI Canvas."
- **"Power and Prediction" (2022) is the sequel: it argues the real payoff comes not from dropping AI into existing tasks ("point solutions") but from redesigning whole "systems" of interdependent decisions ("system solutions") — a slow, contested transition the authors call "the Between Times," analogous to the ~40-year lag between the electric dynamo and the redesigned factory.** Its themes are disruption, decision "power," rules-vs-decisions, and reliability.
- Both books were well received (Prediction Machines named by The Economist one of "the five best books to understand AI"; Power and Prediction a Forbes "10 Best Business Books of 2022"). The most substantive critique, from economist Laurence Kotlikoff in the *Journal of Economic Literature*, is that they never define AI, overstate "AI = prediction," and are complacent about jobs, macroeconomic effects, and state-control risks.

## Key Findings

**The two books share one engine and split the work.** Prediction Machines supplies the microeconomic reframing (cheap prediction; substitutes and complements; judgment and data as complements). Power and Prediction supplies the diffusion/organizational story (why, despite cheap prediction, most industries had not been transformed by 2021, and what it takes to change that). The authors — all at the University of Toronto's Rotman School of Management and co-founders of the Creative Destruction Lab (CDL) — wrote the second book because, in Avi Goldfarb's words, by 2021 they felt a "sense of disappointment" that AI's uses in most companies they interacted with had been "incremental, rather than transformational."

**Prediction Machines' central claim** is that recent AI advances (deep learning) are best understood as a fall in the cost of prediction — "taking information you have to generate information you don't have." Because prediction is an input to decision-making under uncertainty, cheaper prediction means we do more of it, apply it in new places, and raise the value of the things prediction *doesn't* supply: judgment (valuing outcomes) and data.

**Power and Prediction's central claim** is that AI creates disruption at the *system* level, not the task level, and that industry transformation is slow because "system solutions" require changing rules, roles, incentives, and power structures built around expensive prediction. The book's organizing metaphor is factory electrification: cheap electricity mattered not because it lowered power costs but because it eventually allowed a completely redesigned factory.

## Details

### BOOK 1 — "Prediction Machines: The Simple Economics of Artificial Intelligence"

**Publication.** Harvard Business Review Press, first edition April 2018 (ISBN 9781633695672); "Updated and Expanded" edition November 2022 (ISBN 9781647824679). ~250 pp. (2018) / 304 pp. (2022 edition). Audiobook narrated by L.J. Ganser.

**Full structure.** Two framing chapters plus five parts and 19 chapters:
- Ch. 1 Introduction: Machine Intelligence
- Ch. 2 Cheap Changes Everything
- **Part One: Prediction** — 3. Prediction Machine Magic; 4. Why It's Called Intelligence; 5. Data Is the New Oil; 6. The New Division of Labor
- **Part Two: Decision Making** — 7. Unpacking Decisions; 8. The Value of Judgment; 9. Predicting Judgment; 10. Taming Complexity; 11. Fully Automated Decision Making
- **Part Three: Tools** — 12. Deconstructing Work Flows; 13. Decomposing Decisions; 14. Job Redesign
- **Part Four: Strategy** — 15. AI in the C-Suite; 16. When AI Transforms Your Business; 17. Your Learning Strategy; 18. Managing AI Risk
- **Part Five: Society** — 19. Beyond Business

The five parts form a "pyramid": prediction → decision-making → tools → strategy → society.

**Core thesis and argument.** AI is not magic and not (yet) general intelligence; it is prediction technology. Framing it as cheap prediction lets you apply standard economics: when the price of an input falls, you use more of it and more of its complements, and less of its substitutes. Prediction is a substitute for human forecasting (whose value falls) but a complement to human judgment and to data (whose value rises). Decisions therefore get "unbundled" into prediction (increasingly by machines) and judgment (still by humans).

**Key concepts and frameworks.**
- **AI as cheap prediction** — the foundational reframing.
- **The anatomy/building blocks of a decision** — prediction, judgment, action, and outcome, fed by three kinds of data (input, training, feedback). Prediction gives the probabilities of outcomes; judgment assigns value/payoffs to them.
- **The AI Canvas** — a one-page tool (taught to Rotman MBAs) to decompose a decision into its components (action, prediction, judgment, outcome, plus input/training/feedback data) and assess whether an AI is worth building. Introduced in the book and in the authors' April 2018 HBR article "A Simple Tool to Start Making Decisions with the Help of AI."
- **Judgment and data as complements** — as prediction gets cheaper, judgment and data become more valuable.
- **Reward function engineering** — the increasingly valuable human task of specifying the rewards/payoffs (the judgment) that map predictions to actions; "the job of determining the rewards to various actions, given the predictions made by the AI."
- **Prediction by exception** — machines handle routine, high-confidence predictions at scale; humans step in for rare, low-confidence "exception" cases, which raises the value of human judgment.
- **Substitutes and complements / the economics of data** — data and prediction are complements; unique data confers competitive advantage (Google/Facebook sell predictions, not raw data).
- **Impact on jobs/tasks** — AI operates on *tasks*, not whole *jobs*; jobs get redesigned as prediction tasks shift to machines.
- **Known knowns / known unknowns / unknown unknowns** (Rumsfeld, with the authors adding "unknown knowns") — used to bound where machine prediction works; machines are weak on rare events with little historical data.
- **Strategy implications** — cheaper prediction can change not just productivity but strategy itself (scale, scope, vertical integration, timing of investment).

**Signature examples and anecdotes.**
- **The Amazon "shopping-then-shipping → shipping-then-shopping" thought experiment.** If Amazon's recommendation AI became accurate enough (crossing a threshold), it could ship goods before customers order ("anticipatory shipping," a real Amazon patent), invest in a returns-pickup truck fleet, and vertically integrate. The book notes that at today's accuracy customers would return ~95% of shipped items, so the model isn't yet viable — illustrating how "turning the dial" on prediction accuracy can flip a business model.
- **Self-driving/autonomous vehicles** — full automation once prediction (of surroundings/behavior) is good enough.
- **Credit-card fraud detection**, **medical imaging/radiology** (machines front-loading image analysis, humans doing judgment), **translation**, **customer churn prediction**.
- The "**data is the new oil**" chapter.
- An "**I, Robot**"-style illustration of human judgment in life-and-death choices (developed more explicitly in the sequel).

**Practical takeaways for managers.**
- Reframe AI questions as "what prediction would help, and what would we do with it?"
- Use the AI Canvas to decompose decisions and identify high-ROI predictions.
- Invest in judgment and data as the scarce complements.
- Redesign workflows and jobs task-by-task; anticipate "prediction by exception" human-machine division of labor.
- Treat AI as a C-suite strategy issue (scale, scope, timing), and manage new risks (liability, discrimination, data security, being "gamed").

**"Beyond Business" (Part Five).** Societal trade-offs: jobs and inequality (automation can depress some wages while raising returns to capital/judgment), competition and the risk of dominant data-rich firms, privacy vs. performance, and geopolitics (US vs. China in AI).

### BOOK 2 — "Power and Prediction: The Disruptive Economics of Artificial Intelligence"

**Publication.** Harvard Business Review Press, November 15, 2022 (ISBN 9781647824198). ~304 pages.

**Full structure.** Six parts, 18 chapters, plus epilogue:
- **Part One: The Between Times** — 1. A Parable of Three Entrepreneurs; 2. AI's System Future; 3. AI Is Prediction Technology
- **Part Two: Rules** — 4. To Decide or Not to Decide; 5. Hidden Uncertainty; 6. Rules Are Glue
- **Part Three: Systems** — 7. Glued versus Oiled Systems; 8. The System Mindset; 9. The Greatest System of All
- **Part Four: Power** — 10. Disruption and Power; 11. Do Machines Have Power?; 12. Accumulating Power
- **Part Five: How AI Disrupts** — 13. A Great Decoupling; 14. Thinking Probabilistically; 15. The New Judges
- **Part Six: Envisaging New Systems** — 16. Designing Reliable Systems; 17. The Blank Slate; 18. Anticipating System Change
- **Epilogue: AI Bias and Systems**

**Core thesis and argument.** AI's transformative value comes from redesigning *systems* of interdependent decisions, not from improving individual tasks. Most current AI is deployed as "point solutions" that reinforce existing systems, so their benefits are muted. The big gains require "system solutions" that upend existing arrangements — which is disruptive, slow, and politically resisted because it redistributes power. We are in "the Between Times": after AI's capability has been demonstrated, before its widespread adoption.

**Key concepts and frameworks.**
- **The Between Times** — the interval between demonstration of a technology's power and its system-wide adoption; the authors invoke the ~40-year gap between the dynamo (Edison, early 1880s) and the redesigned electric factory (1920s), drawing on Stanford economist Paul David's 1990 "The Dynamo and the Computer: An Historical Perspective on the Modern Productivity Paradox" (echoing Robert Solow's "we see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics").
- **Point solutions vs. application solutions vs. system solutions** (the "parable of three entrepreneurs" using steam→electricity): point = improve an existing task without changing the system; application = a new stand-alone procedure adopted without changing the system; system = redesign of the whole system of decisions. The distinguishing test is *independence*: does the value of the enhanced decision depend on other changes to the system?
- **Decoupling / "A Great Decoupling"** — AI separates prediction from judgment (previously bundled in one human mind), letting each be supplied by different people/machines at different times/places. This is the source of both value creation and power shifts.
- **Rules vs. decisions** — rules are decisions made in advance; they reduce cognitive cost and increase reliability but ignore local information. Cheap prediction raises the relative return to real-time decisions, "liberating" some decisions from rules.
- **"Rules are glue"** — rules bind a system together; a powerful AI dropped into a system built around a rule often adds only marginal value because the surrounding parts resist change (e.g., personalized-learning AI vs. the age-based school curriculum).
- **Glued vs. oiled systems** — "glued" systems are locked into rigid rules; "oiled" systems flexibly adapt to new information/predictions.
- **Hidden uncertainty / scaffolding** — organizations build expensive "scaffolding" to hide the uncertainty that makes their rules wasteful (e.g., airport design accommodating the rule "arrive early").
- **The system mindset** — plan AI adoption by rethinking the whole system rather than substituting labor task-by-task; the dominant "task-level thinking" leads only to point solutions.
- **Power** — defined as *economic* power: you have power if what you own/control is scarce relative to demand. System redesign shifts power at industry, company, and job levels; incumbents who benefit from the status quo resist, setting the stage for disruption.
- **Do machines have power?** — no; "there's no such thing as a machine decision." AI generates predictions; codified human judgment executes actions. AI reallocates power by changing *who* provides judgment and *when/where* decisions are made, and by *scaling* one person's judgment.
- **Thinking probabilistically** — AI converts deterministic systems into probabilistic ones; the authors suggest this may require an "FDA-type" regulatory function to reassure society.
- **Decision rights / decision efficiency ("The New Judges")** — assign decision rights to whoever will decide in the organization's best interest at lowest cost; four factors: information, skills, incentives, coordination.
- **Reliability, coordination, and modularity** — the two main ways to preserve system reliability when introducing AI-based decisions.
- **The AI Systems Discovery Canvas** (Ch. 17, "The Blank Slate") — a three-step tool: (1) articulate the mission; (2) reduce the business to the fewest decisions needed to achieve the mission assuming super-powerful AI; (3) specify the prediction and judgment for each primary decision.
- **First-mover advantage / flywheel** — because AI learns and the marginal cost of a prediction approaches zero, early advantages compound, driving aggressive "racing."
- **AI bias and discrimination** (Epilogue) — point-solution AI can amplify bias, but the authors argue AI should be used in high-stakes decisions *because* it is more scrutable than humans (you can ask an AI thousands of counterfactual questions); University of Chicago's Sendhil Mullainathan is cited that algorithmic discrimination is easier to detect and fix than human ("wetware") bias.

**Signature examples and case studies.**
- **Factory electrification** (the framing parable of three entrepreneurs).
- **The Flint, Michigan water crisis** — University of Michigan researchers Eric Schwartz and Jacob Abernethy built an AI predicting (with roughly 80% accuracy) which homes had lead pipes; local politicians overrode it for a district-by-district "systematic" approach, and the hit rate collapsed to about 15%, until a judge ordered the city to use the predictions again. Illustrates decoupling prediction from judgment, and how prediction can strip power from incumbents who then resist.
- **Covid-19 / social distancing** — reframed as an information problem (blanket rules because we couldn't identify the infectious); an "oiled" system of ~12 companies used rapid antigen testing to make information-based decisions, later adopted by 2,000+ organizations.
- **Verafin** — Canadian fraud/anti-money-laundering detection firm (St. John's, Newfoundland), acquired by Nasdaq for US$2.75 billion (announced Nov. 2020, completed Feb. 2021) — cited as an early beneficiary of cheaper prediction.
- **Insurance/insurtech** — the "blank slate" reduces home insurance to three decisions (marketing, underwriting, claims); a new value proposition is an insurer that *reduces* risk (monitors/fixes leaky pipes) rather than only transferring it.
- **Healthcare** — ER triage (in use at SickKids, Toronto); a superhuman heart-attack-prediction AI could move care from hospital to home; nurses could become more central as diagnosis (a prediction task) is automated.
- **Major League Baseball** automated ball/strike calls — superstar players resisted most effectively because human-umpire bias had favored them (bigger/smaller strike zones), illustrating who resists fairer systems.
- **Advertising** — already disrupted from a "Mad Men" relationship business into an algorithmic one dominated by big tech.
- Also invoked: Netflix, Amazon, Google, Meta, Apple as system-level (not task-level) AI successes; personalized education vs. the age-based curriculum; greenhouses/pest prediction; radiology and the potential centralization of the prediction task.

**Practical takeaways for managers/policymakers.**
- Adopt a *system mindset*; look past point solutions to system redesign, which yields the greatest ROI.
- Use the AI Systems Discovery Canvas: start from the mission and a "blank slate," strip to core decisions, specify prediction + judgment.
- Expect and manage power shifts; incumbents should be willing to disrupt themselves before a startup does.
- Design for reliability (coordination or modularity) and for high-frequency feedback data.
- Move early to capture learning/flywheel advantages.
- Policymakers: reduce regulatory ambiguity, use government's buyer/coordinator role, and design systems to detect and reduce bias.

**Authors' stated reason for the sequel.** By 2021 the authors saw AI's promise unrealized in most companies and concluded the bottleneck was system-level implementation, not the technology — hence a book on disruption, systems, and power. They also explicitly revisit the Amazon example, conceding the first book "underappreciated" that the ship-then-shop threshold required a *system* solution, not a point solution.

### How the two books relate and differ

- **Continuity:** Same economic engine (cheap prediction; judgment/data as complements; decisions as prediction + judgment). Power and Prediction restates these in its Part One before advancing.
- **Shift of focus:** From the *economics of the decision* (Book 1) to the *economics of the system* and *disruption* (Book 2). Book 1 is largely optimistic and firm-internal (tasks, workflows, the AI Canvas); Book 2 is about industry transformation, incumbents vs. entrants, power, and timing.
- **Self-correction:** Power and Prediction explicitly revisits and refines the Amazon example, conceding the earlier book treated as a point solution what is really a system solution.
- **New vocabulary in Book 2:** the Between Times; point/application/system solutions; glued vs. oiled systems; rules-as-glue; the Great Decoupling; the AI Systems Discovery Canvas; decision rights/reliability; economic "power."

### Critical reception

**Prediction Machines.**
- **Praise/endorsements:** Strong blurbs from Hal Varian (Google chief economist), Lawrence Summers, Susan Athey (Stanford), Dominic Barton (McKinsey), and Ruslan Salakhutdinov (Apple). The Economist named it one of "the five best books to understand AI" (a line the publisher features on the updated edition). strategy+business (Theodore Kinni) called it "one of the must-reads on the topic." The Strategy Bridge (2019) gave a positive review extracting three insights for military decision-making (predictions are situation-specific; will sometimes be wrong; decisions still require human judgment). Widely praised for cutting through AI hype with a simple, actionable economic frame.
- **The major critical review — Laurence Kotlikoff (Boston University), *Journal of Economic Literature* 60(3), Sept. 2022, pp. 1052–57, "Does *Prediction Machines* Predict Our AI Future? A Review" (DOI 10.1257/jel.20191519):** Recommends the book strongly ("a must read" for business leaders, economists, and the public) but raises pointed objections: (1) the authors never clearly define AI, effectively saying "We can't say what AI is, but we know it when we see it"; (2) the "AI is all about prediction" thesis is overstated — new prediction-based calculations build on older non-prediction AI; (3) they dismiss the "boring, mundane side of AI" (e.g., digital payments), which he says "the authors poo poo," attributing the blind spot to "the AI incubation lab they run in Toronto, whose firms are surely selected to focus on prediction"; (4) complacency on jobs — a "What, me, worry?" attitude with strained examples of new human jobs; (5) they ignore *macroeconomic* effects and the possibility of long-run "immiseration"; (6) they underplay AI's use for state control over human behavior. Kotlikoff also reframes the book as "actually a terrifying book" about surveillance and manipulation.
- **Reader-level criticism (Goodreads/Audible):** some find it repetitive and lacking technical depth.

**Power and Prediction.**
- **Accolades:** Named one of Forbes' "10 Best Business Books of 2022" (Christian Stadler); a Toronto Star bestseller.
- **Financial Times (review blurb):** "This jauntily written and thought-provoking book sketches out how this new economic revolution might unfold."
- **Forbes (review text):** "makes a strong case for developing system-level AI applications in organizations and institutions."
- **TechTalks (Ben Dickson, Dec. 2022):** favorable, summarizing the point/application/system framework and endorsing the system-level argument.
- **Academic reviews:** Timothy Bresnahan (Stanford) reviewed it in *Business Economics* (Vol. 59, 2023, pp. 120–122) — the review exists but is paywalled and its specific criticisms could not be verified; Ira Sohn reviewed it in *Foresight* (2024); a QRCA Views review (2023) recommends it.
- **Reader-level criticism:** some find it repetitive toward the end, focused on large enterprises over SMEs, and light on AI's ethical risks and on disruption of creative industries.

### Key statistics, data points, and memorable illustrations
- **~40 years** from the dynamo to the electrified/redesigned factory; the 2012 ImageNet/Geoffrey Hinton deep-learning breakthrough as the start of the current AI era. Hinton's widely quoted 2016 remark (at Rotman's Machine Learning and the Market for Intelligence conference): "People should stop training radiologists now. It's just completely obvious that within five years deep learning is going to do better than radiologists" — which had not happened by the book's writing, a point the authors use to motivate the "Between Times."
- The **~95% return rate** that makes Amazon's ship-then-shop model not-yet-viable.
- Flint: **~80%** AI accuracy vs. **~15%** under the politicians' district-based approach.
- System complexity: three binary decisions → 8 combinations; ten → 1,024; twenty → over a million (motivating simulation/digital twins).
- Memorable lines/metaphors: "data is the new oil"; "there's no such thing as a machine decision"; turning up the "volume knob" on prediction accuracy; the "parable of three entrepreneurs."

### Publisher marketing and author media
- Publisher framing: Prediction Machines "recasts the rise of AI as a drop in the cost of prediction"; Power and Prediction promises to help leaders navigate disruption in "the Between Times."
- The authors' HBR articles seeded both books: "How AI Will Change Strategy: A Thought Experiment" (2017, the Amazon example), "A Simple Tool to Start Making Decisions with the Help of AI" (2018, the AI Canvas), and "AI leads to reward function engineering" (Digitopoly/HBR, 2017).

The authors maintain predictionmachines.ai and personal sites (agrawal.ca, joshuagans.com, avigoldfarb.com).

## Recommendations

**Read them as a pair, in order, for different purposes:**
- **Read Prediction Machines first** for the mental model: cheap prediction, complements/substitutes, and the AI Canvas for evaluating a specific AI use case. It is the better *toolkit* for a manager scoping an individual AI project.
- **Read Power and Prediction** for the organizational/strategic question: why AI investments underdeliver, how to spot system-level opportunities, and how power will shift. Use the AI Systems Discovery Canvas for "blank slate" redesign.

**For a summary writer:** Emphasize (1) the single unifying idea (AI = cheap prediction) and (2) the sequel's pivot (point vs. system solutions, the Between Times, power). Foreground the two concrete tools — the AI Canvas (Book 1) and the AI Systems Discovery Canvas (Book 2). Use the Amazon, Flint, Covid, and factory-electrification examples as the memorable anchors. Present the Kotlikoff review as the substantive counterweight to the largely positive reception.

**Thresholds that would change the recommendation:** If you need hands-on technical/ML detail, neither book suffices (both are deliberately non-technical) — pair them with a technical text. If your interest is AI safety/existential risk or labor economics in depth, treat these books as starting points and supplement with the critical literature (e.g., Kotlikoff's macroeconomic concerns).

## Caveats
- **Editions:** "Prediction Machines" exists in a 2018 first edition and a 2022 "Updated and Expanded" edition; page counts and some framing differ. The 19-chapter, five-part structure is stable across editions; the update adds a reset preface/context rather than reordering chapters.
- **Chapter titles** are verified against a library catalog record and the publisher/O'Reilly table of contents; minor wording ("Work Flows" vs. "Workflows") varies by source.
- **Some examples and figures** are drawn from the authors' own interviews/articles and reputable secondary summaries rather than the books' full text (per the instruction not to reproduce long passages). The Flint accuracy figures (~80% → ~15%) come from reporting on the underlying project and the authors' account; the ~95% Amazon return figure appears in a book excerpt.
- **Bresnahan's academic review** of Power and Prediction (Business Economics, 2023) could not be accessed (paywalled); its specific criticisms are unverified.
- **The "ship-then-shop never materialized" point** is well supported by logistics-industry sources but is an inference about the example, not a criticism made by a named book reviewer.
- Publisher blurbs and "best books" lines are marketing-selected quotations; the FT and Forbes lines for Power and Prediction are review comments/list placements, and the FT quote specifically is a review blurb rather than a formal FT "best books" listing.
